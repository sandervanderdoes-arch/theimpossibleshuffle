            ✓ Provably fair: check any shuffle yourself

            Each shuffle is cooked from three ingredients: the randomness of a public drand round (published every 30 seconds by the League of Entropy, so nobody could know it in advance), a seat (your browser's id, so nobody can hand in your shuffles) and a number (1st, 2nd, 3rd shuffle with that round). Those go through SHA-256, and the 256-bit result picks the order of all 52 cards (Fisher–Yates). Same recipe, same deck, on any computer. Faking a winning shuffle means trying recipes until one works, which is just shuffling the long way round.

            Is every order equally likely? As good as. SHA-256 gives 2256 possible results and there are 52! possible decks, so every deck can come up. But 2256 doesn't divide evenly by 52!: each deck gets about 1,435,590,242 of the results, and some get one more. So some decks are about 1 in 1.4 billion more likely than others. Over the lifetime of the universe, nobody could ever notice.

            Can't I just run the recipe on my own computer? Yes. That's called shuffling. Welcome aboard. It's literally what Time Travellers pay us to do. You can't work ahead (nobody knows a drand round before it's published), you can't hand in someone else's shuffles, and the database rebuilds every claimed shuffle before it believes it. A fast graphics card might win you a spot on the Luckiest Shufflers board. The Holy Grail stays 1 in 8 × 1067.
